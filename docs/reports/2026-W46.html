Redundancy maximization as a principle of associative memory learning in Hopfield networks.

Blümel Mark · Schneider Andreas C · Neuhaus Valentin · Ehrlich David A · Graetz Marcel · Wibral Michael · et al.

Associative memory, traditionally modeled by Hopfield networks, enables the retrieval of previously stored patterns from partial or noisy cues. Yet, the local computational principles which are required to enable this function remain incompletely understood. To formally characterize the local information processing in such systems, we employ a recent extension of information theory-Partial Information Decomposition. It decomposes the contribution of different inputs to an output into unique information from each input, redundant information across inputs, and synergistic information that emerges from combining different inputs. Applying this framework to individual neurons in classical Hopfield networks we find that below the memory capacity, the information in a neuron's activity is characterized by high redundancy between the external pattern input and the internal recurrent input, while synergy and unique information are close to zero until the memory capacity is surpassed and performance drops steeply. Inspired by this observation, we use redundancy maximization at each neuron as an information-theoretic learning goal. This increases the network's memory capacity to 1.59, a more than tenfold improvement over the 0.14 capacity of classical Hopfield networks, and also outperforming recent state-of-the-art implementations of Hopfield networks. Overall, this work establishes redundancy maximization as a design principle for associative memories and opens pathways for new associative memory models based on information-theoretic goals.

Phase-separated SOX5 drives human cranial neural crest fate commitment.

Huang Xiaochao · Zhong Yi · Wang Kun · Li Yaoxiang · Du Li · Chen Hanyi · et al.

Defective development of multipotent cranial neural crest cells (CNCCs) underlies diverse human neurocristopathies; yet, the mechanisms governing human CNCC fate commitment remain elusive. Using a human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC)-derived CNCC model, we generate transcriptomic, chromatin-accessibility, and transcription-factor-wide (TFome) CRISPR screening datasets, identifying SOX5 as a molecular switch for human CNCC specification. SOX5 loss retains the cells in a precursor-like state and strongly compromises their progression toward migratory CNCCs. Mechanistically, SOX5 forms nuclear condensates with properties consistent with liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) and compartmentalizes active chromatin and their three-dimensional organization. Through a prion-like domain (PLD), SOX5 self-associates and assembles a multiprotein hub containing the chromatin factor KU80, promoting a pro-CNCC program including ZNF467, EGR1, and SOX11. Disrupting the PLD shifts condensates to a more liquid-like state and disrupts KU80 recruitment. These findings uncover an LLPS-associated mechanism in human CNCC fate control, shedding light into the molecular etiology of neurodevelopmental disorders.

Photonic entanglement swapping with remote quantum dots.

Beccaceci Mattia · Ronco Giuseppe · Cienzo Fabrizio · Bassetti Pierpaolo · Laneve Alessandro · Basset Francesco Basso · et al.

Entanglement swapping is a protocol that details how to create entanglement between previously uncorrelated particles. Its photonic version, mediated by the interference of photon pairs generated by separate quantum systems, finds disparate applications in quantum networks. So far, entanglement swapping with entangled photons from remote systems has been implemented only using probabilistic sources. However, the scaling up of quantum networks requires nonprobabilistic quantum emitters without a trade-off between the degree of entanglement and photon-pair generation rate. Here, we demonstrate photonic entanglement swapping using photon pairs generated by two separate GaAs quantum dots. The emitters are deterministically embedded in hybrid semiconductor-piezoelectric devices that make the entangled photons from two dissimilar quantum dots nearly identical. Entanglement swapping is demonstrated with a fidelity as high as 0.71(2), more than 10 SDs above the classical limit. The experimental data are quantitatively explained by a theoretical model that also suggests how to boost the protocol performance. Our work opens the path to the exploitation of quantum dot entangled-photon sources in quantum repeater networks.

PINN-based functional inference in size-structured population models: Application to fish oogenesis.

Fostier Louis · Lesage Manon · Thermes Violette · Clément Frédérique · Yvinec Romain

Early oogenesis in juvenile fish establishes the ovarian reserve, which conditions lifelong reproductive capacity. This process is regulated by local hormonal feedback mechanisms, mainly involving Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH). In this work, we develop a mechanistic size-structured population model describing an unstructured germ-cell compartment and a population of growing oocytes, with AMH feedback on the renewal of the precursor cells compartment. Using repeated cross-sectional observations of oocyte size distributions in fish ovaries, we formulate and solve a nonlinear inverse problem with Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) to infer three demographic functions: the size-dependent oocyte growth rate, the AMH-regulated net renewal rate of the precursor cells compartment, and the recruitment rate of new oocytes. We introduce an auxiliary PINN formulation to handle the nonlocal interaction term and jointly infer unobserved state variables and demographic functions. Our inference strategy combines non-parametric PINNs with a data-guided model-selection step: demographic functions are first inferred without prescribing their functional forms, and the learned profiles are then used to select parsimonious parametric laws, whose parameters are subsequently estimated within a parametric PINN. We investigate identifiability at both the functional and parametric levels, combining structural identifiability analysis with synthetic-data experiments assessing the practical recovery of functions and parameters. The inference procedure is embedded within an individual-level bootstrap framework to assess sensitivity to repeated-cross-sectional sampling and stochastic training. Applied to juvenile fish oogenesis, the framework recovers interpretable growth and renewal dynamics, provides access to unobserved quantities, and enables conditional in silico investigation. More broadly, this work provides a framework for functional and parametric inference in size-structured population models with nonlocal interactions from sparse cross-sectional data.

The resource elasticity of control.

Solomyak Levi · Emanuel Aviv · Eldar Eran

The ability to determine how much the environment can be controlled through our actions has long been viewed as fundamental to adaptive behavior. While traditional accounts treat controllability as a fixed property of the environment, we argue that real-world controllability often depends on the effort, time, and money we are able and willing to invest. In such cases, controllability can be said to be elastic to invested resources. Here, we propose that inferring this elasticity is essential for efficient resource allocation, and thus, elasticity misestimations result in maladaptive behavior. To test this hypothesis, we developed a novel treasure hunt game where participants encountered environments with varying degrees of controllability and elasticity. Across two pre-registered studies (N=514), we first demonstrate that people infer elasticity and adapt their resource allocation accordingly. We then present a computational model that explains how people make this inference and identify individual elasticity biases that lead to suboptimal resource allocation. Finally, we show that overestimation of elasticity is associated with elevated psychopathology involving an impaired sense of control. These findings establish the elasticity of control as a distinct cognitive construct guiding adaptive behavior and a computational marker for control-related maladaptive behavior.
